Lag sequential analysis (LSA) studies the order in which categorical events occur (Sackett, 1979; Bakeman & Gottman, 1997). A process is coded as a sequence of states, such as weekly engagement levels or the moves of a collaborative discussion, and the analysis asks whether one state is followed by another more often, or less often, than the base rates of the two states alone would predict. A transition that occurs more often than expected is a sequential regularity of the process; a transition that occurs less often is an avoidance. Neither can be read from frequencies alone: a frequent transition may only reflect two busy states, and a rare transition may still occur far more often than chance allows.

The method reduces the data to a lagged contingency table and tests every cell of it against sequential independence, the model in which the next state carries no memory of the current one. The result is a set of tested transitions, each with a direction, a size, and a p-value, together with an omnibus test of the whole table. lagdynamics fits the model in one call, lsa(), and returns every part of it as a tidy data frame.

Data

engagement records the weekly engagement state of 138 students over 15 weeks, one row per student and one column per week.

  • Sequence: each row is one student’s sequence.
  • Order: the column order (weeks 1 to 15) orders the states.
  • State: each cell is Active, Average, or Disengaged; NA marks a missing week and is skipped rather than counted as a state.

The lagged contingency table

Let the process visit \(K\) states. At lag \(\ell\), every pair of positions \(\ell\) steps apart within a sequence is one transition, and the transitions are tabulated in a \(K \times K\) table whose cell \(O_{ij}\) counts the occasions on which state \(i\) was followed, \(\ell\) positions later, by state \(j\). At lag 1 this is the table of immediate successors. Pairs never cross the boundary between two sequences, so the last week of one student and the first week of the next are not counted.

Two descriptive quantities come directly from the table. The transitional probability \(P(j \mid i) = O_{ij} / R_i\), where \(R_i\) is the row total, is the share of moves out of state \(i\) that reach state \(j\); it describes where the process goes. The expected count under independence,

\[E_{ij} = \frac{R_i \, C_j}{N},\]

with \(C_j\) the column total and \(N\) the number of transitions, is the count the cell would reach if the next state were drawn from the base rates regardless of the current one.

Adjusted residuals and Yule’s Q

The adjusted residual standardises the gap between the observed and the expected count (Haberman, 1973),

\[z_{ij} = \frac{O_{ij} - E_{ij}} {\sqrt{E_{ij}\,(1 - R_i/N)\,(1 - C_j/N)}},\]

and is approximately standard normal under independence. A residual above 1.96 marks a transition that is over-represented at the 5% level, and a residual below -1.96 one that is under-represented. The residual is the evidential unit of LSA: it states how far a transition departs from chance on a common scale, whatever the base rates of its states.

The residual is a test statistic, so it grows with the number of transitions. Yule’s Q is the matching effect size. For each cell the table is collapsed to a 2-by-2 table (from \(i\) or not, to \(j\) or not) with cells \(a = O_{ij}\), \(b\), \(c\), and \(d\), and

\[Q_{ij} = \frac{ad - bc}{ad + bc},\]

which lies between -1 (the transition never occurs) and 1 (state \(i\) is always followed by state \(j\)), with 0 at independence. Because it does not grow with sample size, Q is the scale on which transitions of different data sets or groups are compared (Bakeman & Quera, 2011).

Fitting the model

lsa() takes the wide data frame directly: rows are sequences and columns are ordered positions. The default is the classical engine at lag 1.

fit <- lsa(engagement)
fit
#> Lag Sequential Analysis  -  classical  (lag 1, directed)
#>   3 states | 1734 transitions | 1870 events | 136 sequences
#>   states: Active, Average, Disengaged
#>   independence: G² = 618.3, df = 4, p <2e-16
#> 
#>   Significant transitions (p < 0.05): 7 of 9
#>   strongest over-represented (of 3):
#>     Active -> Active          z =  +21.7  ***
#>     Disengaged -> Disengaged  z =  +15.4  ***
#>     Average -> Average        z =  +12.5  ***
#> 
#>   Initial states:
#>     Active     0.382  ████████████████████████
#>     Average    0.368  ███████████████████████
#>     Disengaged 0.250  ████████████████

The model counts 1734 transitions among 1870 events in 136 sequences. The omnibus likelihood-ratio test of independence is \(G^2 = 618.3\) on 4 degrees of freedom, so the next week’s state depends strongly on the current one. Seven of the nine transitions depart from independence, and the three strongest over-represented transitions are the three self-transitions.

Which transitions depart from chance

transitions() returns one row per ordered pair with the observed and expected counts, the transitional probability (prob), the adjusted residual (adj_res), its p-value, Yule’s Q, and further association measures. The argument significant = TRUE keeps the transitions whose p-value is below alpha, and sort = "strength" orders them by the absolute residual.

transitions(fit, significant = TRUE, sort = "strength")

Active -> Active is the strongest regularity: it occurred 459 times against 247 expected, an adjusted residual of 21.7 and a Yule’s Q of 0.828. Active students therefore stay active from one week to the next far more often than the base rate of activity predicts. Disengaged -> Disengaged (z = 15.4, Q = 0.754) and Average -> Average (z = 12.5, Q = 0.553) are the other two persistences, so every state tends to repeat. The avoidances are as informative. Active -> Disengaged occurred 23 times against 121 expected (z = -12.6, Q = -0.826): an active student almost never drops to disengagement in a single week. Disengaged -> Active is also avoided (z = -10.5, Q = -0.698), so recovery from disengagement to full activity is rare. Moves between Average and Disengaged, by contrast, occur at the rate the base rates predict.

The two transitions missing from this table, Average -> Disengaged and Disengaged -> Average, are the ones that occur at the rate chance predicts. direction = "over" or direction = "under" keeps only one sign, and min_count drops pairs with few observations.

Four transitions are significantly avoided, and all four connect Active with another state, in either direction. Active engagement is therefore a closed state: students who hold it keep it, and students outside it rarely reach it in one week (Average -> Active, z = -12.9).

Is the table as a whole dependent

tests() returns the omnibus tests of independence for the whole table: the likelihood-ratio statistic (lrx2) and Pearson’s chi-squared (x2).

tests(fit)
#>   test statistic df         p
#> 1 lrx2       618  4 1.69e-132
#> 2   x2       629  4 7.41e-135

Both statistics reject independence (\(G^2 = 618\) and \(\chi^2 = 629\) on 4 degrees of freedom, p far below 0.001). A non-significant omnibus test would give no basis for interpreting individual residuals, so this test is read before the transitions.

States and starting points

nodes() returns one row per state with the number of transitions it sends and receives, and initial() the share of sequences that start in each state.

nodes(fit)
#>        state outgoing incoming
#> 1     Active      658      651
#> 2    Average      751      763
#> 3 Disengaged      325      320
initial(fit)
#>        state init_prob
#> 1     Active     0.382
#> 2    Average     0.368
#> 3 Disengaged     0.250

Average is the busiest state (751 outgoing, 763 incoming transitions) and Disengaged the least visited (325 and 320). These totals are what the expected counts control for: Average is not credited with regularities on the strength of its volume. Sequences start in Active (0.382) and Average (0.368) more often than in Disengaged (0.250).

Transition probabilities

transition_probabilities() returns the transitional probabilities \(P(\text{to} \mid \text{from})\) as a tidy table, one row per ordered pair. They are the edge weights of a transition network analysis model and the input that probability-based tools expect.

transition_probabilities(fit)
#>         from         to  prob
#> 1     Active     Active 0.698
#> 2     Active    Average 0.267
#> 3     Active Disengaged 0.035
#> 4    Average     Active 0.204
#> 5    Average    Average 0.610
#> 6    Average Disengaged 0.186
#> 7 Disengaged     Active 0.120
#> 8 Disengaged    Average 0.397
#> 9 Disengaged Disengaged 0.483

Each state is most often followed by itself: 0.698 for Active, 0.610 for Average, and 0.483 for Disengaged. The probabilities and the residuals answer different questions. Disengaged -> Average has a probability of 0.397, the second largest move out of Disengaged, yet its residual (-1.7) is not significant: the move is frequent because Average is a busy state, not because disengaged students are drawn to it.

Residual heatmap

plot() draws a residual heatmap by default. Rows are the current states, columns the next states, and the fill is the adjusted residual on a diverging scale.

plot(fit)

The heatmap shows the structure of the model at a glance: the diagonal is uniformly over-represented, the four off-diagonal cells in the Active row and column are avoided, and the two cells linking Average and Disengaged sit near zero. The process is one of persistence, with movement mostly confined to the two lower states.
