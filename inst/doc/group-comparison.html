Comparing groups

Written by Mohammed Saqr and Sonsoles López-Pernas

  • Data

  • A permutation test of group differences
  • Barrel plot
  • A Bayesian comparison
  • When to use which

Groups of learners, conditions, or courses can differ in the order of their actions: one group may move from discussion to consensus where another moves from discussion to planning. A lag sequential model fitted to each group describes its transitions, but two models estimated separately almost always look different, if only because of sampling variation. The question a group comparison answers is whether a difference in a transition, or in the transition structure as a whole, is larger than would arise if group membership carried no information about the sequences.

lagdynamics answers it in two steps. lsa(data, group = ) fits one model per group in a shared state space, so the groups can be compared cell by cell even when one group never visits a state. compare_lsa() then tests the differences by permuting group labels over whole sequences, and bayes_compare_lsa() gives the Bayesian counterpart, a credible interval for each difference in transition probability.

Data

group_regulation_long is a long event log of collaborative regulation actions, one row per action: 27,533 actions. Two achievement groups contribute about equally: 13,721 actions from high achievers and 13,812 from low achievers.

  • Actor: Actor identifies the person whose actions form one sequence.
  • Action: Action, the coded regulation action (9 states: adapt, cohesion, consensus, coregulate, discuss, emotion, monitor, plan, synthesis).
  • Time: Time, the timestamp that orders the actions; a gap of more than 900 seconds (the default time_threshold) starts a new sequence.
  • Group: Achiever, High or Low.

Group and Course are further metadata and are not used here.

One model per group

Naming the grouping column in group fits one model per group under the same state labels, engine, and lag.

gfit <- lsa(group_regulation_long, actor = "Actor", action = "Action",
            time = "Time", group = "Achiever")
gfit
#> <lsa_group>
#>   engine:    classical
#>   states:    9 (adapt, cohesion, consensus, coregulate, discuss, emotion, monitor, plan, synthesis)
#>   groups:    2
#>     - High:        1000 sequences
#>     - Low:         1000 sequences

The log yields 1000 sequences in each group over the same 9 states, so the two models have the same cells and can be compared one transition at a time.

A permutation test of group differences

compare_lsa() shuffles the group labels of whole sequences, refits both models, and sets each observed difference against the differences produced by the shuffles. Permuting whole sequences keeps the dependence between the actions of one sequence, so only the link between group and transition structure is tested. The compared measure is by default the log odds ratio of each transition against all other transitions, which does not grow with the number of transitions observed; diff is the high-achiever value minus the low-achiever value. Transitions with a combined count below min_count (default 5) are left untested. The call below draws 500 permutations, so the smallest attainable p-value is 1/501, and adjust = "BH" corrects the per-transition p-values for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The seed is set once, before the permutations.

set.seed(2026)
cmp <- compare_lsa(gfit, R = 500, adjust = "BH")
cmp
#> <lsa_comparison>
#>   groups:   High vs Low
#>   measure:  log_or difference (High - Low)
#>   R:        500 label permutations
#>   edges:    38 significant of 78 tested (adjust = BH)
#>   omnibus:  statistic = 79.48, p = 0.001996

Of the 81 transitions, 78 have enough observations to be tested; the other three have a combined count below 5 and carry NA p-values. After the Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment, 38 of the 78 differ between the groups. The omnibus test, the sum of squared differences over the tested transitions, has a statistic of 79.48 and p = 0.001996, the smallest value 500 permutations can give: no relabelling of the sequences produced a difference as large as the observed one, so the transition structures of the two groups differ.

The omnibus test is read as a table with what = "global".

as.data.frame(cmp, what = "global")
#>   group_a group_b statistic p_value   R
#> 1    High     Low      79.5   0.002 500

The global table has one row per pair of groups, with the statistic, its permutation p-value, and the number of permutations, the form to report alongside the per-transition results.

transitions() reads the per-transition differences. significant = TRUE keeps the transitions whose Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value (p_adj) is below 0.05, and sort = "strength" orders them by the absolute difference.

The p-values in p_adj are Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted. The largest differences in log odds ratio concern transitions into adapt and synthesis. cohesion -> adapt has a log odds ratio of -0.794 among high achievers and -3.921 among low achievers (difference 3.13, adjusted p = 0.00599): in the low group, cohesion is followed by adaptation far less than base rates predict, whereas in the high group the avoidance is mild. emotion -> synthesis (2.90) and plan -> synthesis (2.81) follow the same pattern: high achievers reach synthesis from emotional and planning moves that low achievers almost never convert into synthesis. In the other direction, synthesis -> monitor (-2.25) is relatively more frequent among low achievers.

Barrel plot

plot() draws a group barrel plot: for the most frequent transitions, the transition probability of each group is drawn back to back, with the result of the permutation test on each transition.

plot(cmp)

The 12 most frequent transitions are shown, ordered by frequency, with the high achievers on the left and the low achievers on the right; each bar is labelled with its count and the centre with the adjusted p-value, and 7 of the 12 differ significantly. High achievers move from discuss to consensus about twice as often as low achievers (851 against 418 transitions) and from consensus back to discuss likewise (789 against 401), while low achievers repeat plan more often (1356 against 948). The most frequent transition, consensus -> plan, does not differ (adjusted p = 0.097), nor do emotion -> cohesion or cohesion -> consensus: the groups share the backbone of the process and differ in how discussion connects to consensus and planning.

A Bayesian comparison

bayes_compare_lsa() asks a different question: given the counts, what is the plausible range of the true difference between the two groups’ transition probabilities? Each state’s outgoing transitions have a Dirichlet posterior in each group, and the comparison reports the posterior mean difference, its 95% credible interval, and the probability of direction (pd). A transition is credibly different when its interval excludes zero and the difference is not negligible. No sequences are resampled, so the comparison is fast, but it treats transitions as independent draws. adjust = "BH" applies the Benjamini-Hochberg correction to the two-sided Bayesian p-value (p_adj); the significant flag itself is decided by the credible interval. seed fixes the Monte Carlo draws.

bcmp <- bayes_compare_lsa(gfit, adjust = "BH", seed = 1)
bcmp

#>   groups:    High vs Low
#>   prior:     Dirichlet(0.50)  |  draws: 10000  |  CI: 95%
#>   edges:     38 credibly different of 81 compared

The Bayesian comparison finds 38 of 81 transitions credibly different. The largest is discuss -> consensus: a posterior probability of 0.424 among high achievers and 0.214 among low achievers, a difference of 0.210 with a credible interval from 0.182 to 0.238. synthesis -> consensus differs by 0.186, but its interval (0.109 to 0.260) is wider because synthesis is a rarely visited source state.

The two comparisons do not flag the same transitions, because they compare different quantities. synthesis -> adapt illustrates the difference: its posterior transition probability is 0.143 among high achievers and 0.300 among low achievers, a credible difference of -0.16 (interval -0.218 to -0.094). synthesis -> adapt is among them: its log odds ratio is 3.002 among high achievers and 2.952 among low achievers (adjusted p = 0.869). The association between the two states, relative to their base rates, is the same in both groups; the larger probability among low achievers follows from a higher base rate of adapt in that group, not from a stronger link between synthesis and adaptation. The Bayesian comparison answers how large a difference in transition probability is; the permutation test on the log odds ratio answers whether the sequential association itself differs.

When to use which

  • Describe each group: lsa(data, group = ), then summary() and transitions() on the grouped fit.
  • Test whether groups differ, transition by transition and overall: compare_lsa() with an explicit adjust. It respects the dependence within sequences and can compare odds ratios, residuals, Yule’s Q, or probabilities (measure).
  • Estimate how large a difference in transition probability is: bayes_compare_lsa(), which reports a credible interval for every difference without resampling.
  • More than two groups: both functions compare every pair and apply the correction across all pairs.

A difference between groups can also arise from a difference in their composition, such as the courses each group comes from; when such a confound is plausible, compare within subgroups. The checks for a single model are in Evaluating transitions, and the method itself in Lag sequential analysis.
